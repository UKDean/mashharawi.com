Steel Weight Verification: guide

How to compare the measured weight of a rebar sample with its nominal weight, and how to read the deviation without over-reading it.

What the tool does

It calculates the theoretical (nominal) weight of a sample from its diameter, length and number of bars, and compares it with what the sample actually weighed: the difference in kilograms and the deviation in percent. It does not decide whether steel is acceptable.

When to use it

  • Receiving inspection on site or in the store.
  • Checking a delivery against nominal weight before discussing an invoice.
  • Comparing several samples from one batch.

Step by step

1. Measure the sample

  1. Cut a straight piece, square at both ends.
  2. Measure its length with a tape or rule (to the millimetre if you can).
  3. Weigh it on a calibrated scale; for small samples use a scale with fine resolution.
  4. Weigh several bars together if you want, and count them.

2. Enter the values

  • Diameter: tap a size (10–32 mm) or type another; other sizes are marked non-standard.
  • Grade: for your records only. Grade 60 and Grade 80 have the same nominal weight.
  • Sample length: type the number and choose mm or m. The tool keeps your unit and never guesses; 1000 mm and 1 m are the same. Any positive length up to 12 m is accepted.
  • Number of bars weighed and measured total weight in kg, with a dot for decimals.
  • Reference and notes are optional.

3. Optionally enter your range

If your specification or contract gives a weight range, enter its lower limit (zero or negative, for example −4) and upper limit (zero or positive). Each sample is then shown as inside or outside that range. This is only a comparison with your numbers. The tool has no built-in limits and gives no compliance decision.

4. Add the sample

The result appears as you type. Press Add sample to keep it in the session table, then enter the next one.

Worked example

One sample

16 mm, three bars of 1.000 m, measured 4.600 kg.

  1. Unit weight = 0.006165 × 16² = 1.57824 kg/m.
  2. Theoretical = 1.57824 × 1.000 × 3 = 4.73472 kg.
  3. Difference = 4.600 − 4.73472 = −0.13472 kg.
  4. Deviation = −0.13472 ÷ 4.73472 × 100 = −2.85%: the sample is lighter than nominal.

Reading the results

  • Deviation sign. Positive: heavier than nominal. Negative: lighter.
  • Sample deviation belongs to one sample. Pooled deviation treats all samples of one diameter as one combined weighing; it is shown beside the lowest and highest sample so no individual result is hidden. It is not an average of percentages.
  • Total masses across all samples are given in kilograms only. Different diameters are never combined into one percentage.
  • Input check. A difference over 25% shows a neutral note asking you to re-check the unit, bars and diameter. It is not an acceptance limit.
  • Inside / outside the range you entered appears only if you entered a range.

Why a deviation alone does not prove non-compliance

Acceptance depends on the governing standard and the contract: how many samples, how they are taken, how long they are, the measuring and weighing accuracy, and the limits that apply. A single weighing with a tape and a site scale cannot settle that. The result is a theoretical comparison, not a mill test certificate (MTC), not a laboratory test and not a certification. Use it to decide whether to ask for the MTC or a proper test.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing m but typing millimetres (or the opposite): the input check will usually flag it.
  • Weighing several bars but entering 1 as the number of bars.
  • Bent or uneven ends making the measured length too long or short.
  • Using a scale that is too coarse for small samples.
  • Reading a deviation as a pass or fail.

Limitations

  • Nominal weight only, from 0.006165 × d².
  • No built-in tolerances or standards.
  • The session is not saved; download the CSV or print it.
  • Accuracy depends on your measurement and scale.
